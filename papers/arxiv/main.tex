\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems increasingly add iterative and
multi-agent control loops, but comparisons across these architectures are
often confounded by differences in corpora, budgets, logging, and metrics.
This paper presents \textbf{Agentic Retrieval Systems (ARS)}, an open-source
harness that runs conventional retrieve-once RAG, a budgeted single-agent
iterative retriever, and a role-separated multi-agent pipeline against a
shared corpus, model interface, metric suite, and budget controller, and
records per-question traces.
The three architectures are compared with \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} on a
153-question slice of the HotpotQA distractor development set, using BM25
over 5{,}675 sentence-level passages, under unconstrained and
equal-retrieval budgets, which give the same conclusions.
With the default five passages for RAG, the multi-agent pipeline is more
accurate under an audited LLM judge (0.765 vs.\ 0.601; paired difference
$+0.163$, 95\% CI $+0.098$ to $+0.235$) at 8.2$\times$ the tokens, while the
single-agent loop gives no measurable gain.
The multi-agent pipeline, however, reads about 13 unique passages per
question.
A control that changes only RAG's \texttt{top\_k} to 15 raises RAG to 0.752
and leaves a multi-agent advantage of $+0.013$ ($-0.033$ to $+0.059$), with
RAG using 30\% of the multi-agent tokens and about 1\,s instead of 43\,s per
question.
In this setting the multi-agent gain is therefore explained almost entirely
by the larger evidence budget rather than by planning and critique.
The study is limited to one model, a small derived slice, and a local BM25
corpus.
Code, configurations, run artifacts, and analysis scripts are available at
\url{https://github.com/benkarlsberg/Agentic-Retrieval-Systems}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Knowledge-intensive question answering increasingly relies on
retrieval-augmented generation
\citep{lewis2020rag,karpukhin2020dpr,gao2023ragsurvey}.
Beyond retrieve-once pipelines, recent systems interleave retrieval with
reasoning \citep{trivedi2023ircot,jiang2023flare,yao2023react}, critique
their evidence \citep{asai2023selfrag}, route queries by complexity
\citep{jeong2024adaptiverag}, or split planning, retrieval, critique, and
synthesis across specialized agents
\citep{nguyen2025marag,singh2025agenticrag}.
These designs target multi-hop questions, where a single retrieval round can
miss part of the evidence, but they add model calls, tokens, and latency.
Because each system is usually evaluated with its own corpus, budgets, and
metrics, an observed quality gap may reflect implementation choices, or
simply a larger amount of retrieved text, rather than the control
architecture.

ARS holds the corpus, retriever, model, budget controller, and metrics fixed
across three architectures and reports process cost next to answer quality.
Its research questions are whether bounded iterative retrieval (RQ1) or
role-separated multi-agent retrieval (RQ2) improves on retrieve-once RAG
enough to justify the overhead, how the architectures handle unanswerable
questions (RQ3), and how rankings depend on the retriever (RQ4).
This paper addresses RQ1 and RQ2, gives preliminary evidence on RQ3
(8 items), and leaves RQ4 open.
The contributions are the harness itself; a live comparison on a
153-question HotpotQA-derived slice under two budget modes, with paired
bootstrap intervals, per-stratum results, and an LLM judge checked against a
manual audit; a passage-budget control showing that RAG given as many
passages as the multi-agent pipeline reads matches its accuracy at a fraction
of the cost; and an open release of code, run artifacts, and the script that
regenerates every table and figure.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Retrieval and iterative retrieval.}
Retrieve-and-read pipelines \citep{chen2017drqa}, dense retrieval
\citep{karpukhin2020dpr}, and RAG \citep{lewis2020rag} are standard
baselines; BM25 remains strong \citep{robertson2009bm25}, and reciprocal rank
fusion is a common hybrid \citep{cormack2009rrf}.
IRCoT interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning
\citep{trivedi2023ircot}, FLARE retrieves when generation confidence is low
\citep{jiang2023flare}, Self-RAG learns when to retrieve and how to critique
evidence \citep{asai2023selfrag}, Adaptive-RAG routes by estimated complexity
\citep{jeong2024adaptiverag}, and tool-using agents couple reasoning with
search actions \citep{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer,press2023selfask}.
ARS proposes no new policy; it provides a controlled setting for comparing
such policies.
Long contexts are not free: language models use information in the middle of
long inputs less reliably \citep{liu2024lost}, which is one reason a larger
\texttt{top\_k} might hurt rather than help.

\paragraph{Agentic RAG comparisons and harnesses.}
A recent survey organizes agentic RAG by agent cardinality, control
structure, and autonomy, and names evaluation and efficiency as open
problems \citep{singh2025agenticrag}.
MA-RAG assigns planning, step definition, evidence extraction, and answering
to separate agents and reports gains on multi-hop benchmarks including
HotpotQA \citep{nguyen2025marag}.
\citet{ferrazzi2026agenticrag} find neither enhanced RAG pipelines nor a
single-tool agentic system universally better on four datasets, with the
agentic system using about 3.3$\times$ more input tokens and 1.5$\times$ more
time, and \citet{mccleary2026bcas} measure how search depth, retrieval
strategy, and completion budget affect the accuracy and cost of agentic
search across six LLMs.
FlashRAG reimplements 16 RAG methods over 38 datasets \citep{jin2025flashrag},
and RAG Playground compares retrieval variants combined with ReAct agents
\citep{papadimitriou2024ragplayground}.
ARS complements these works by fixing the corpus, retriever, model, and
budget controller across three control architectures, logging tokens and
latency for every call, matching passage budgets, reporting paired bootstrap
intervals, and releasing the traces.
HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, Natural Questions
\citep{kwiatkowski2019nq}, and 2WikiMultiHopQA \citep{ho2020twowiki} are the
usual multi-hop and open-domain benchmarks.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:design}

The three runners share the corpus, retriever, model client, budget
controller, and metric suite.
\textbf{Conventional RAG} issues one retrieval call with the original
question, returning \texttt{top\_k} passages, and one generation call that
answers or abstains.
The \textbf{bounded single-agent} runner makes a controller call at each step
that chooses to \emph{retrieve}, \emph{reformulate}, \emph{stop} (and
synthesize), or \emph{abstain}; retrieved passages are deduplicated, and hard
caps on steps, model calls, retrieval calls, documents, and tokens bound the
loop.
In the \textbf{multi-agent} pipeline a planner call decomposes the question,
two retriever roles (``fact'' and ``evidence'') alternate over up to three
retrieval rounds that exclude passages already retrieved, a critic call after
each round judges whether the evidence suffices, and a synthesizer call
produces the answer or an abstention.

Available retrievers are BM25, dense retrieval over hashing-based fixture
embeddings, reciprocal-rank-fusion hybrids, and a lexical reranker; the live
study uses BM25 only.
Budgets cap steps, model calls, documents, tokens, retrieval calls, and
\texttt{top\_k}.
The \texttt{unconstrained} mode applies the configured caps, the
\texttt{equal\_retrieval} mode limits every architecture to 3 retrieval calls
and 15 documents (RAG to one retrieval and one model call), and an
\texttt{equal\_token} mode (not run here) imposes a shared token cap.
Every question emits JSONL events with queries, retrieved passage IDs and
scores, routing actions, API token counts, latency, and termination reason; a
deterministic heuristic model supports offline tests
(Appendix~\ref{app:validation}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data and corpus.}
The evaluation slice contains 153 questions drawn with seed 42 from the
HotpotQA distractor development set (7{,}405 questions): 100 bridge
questions (the \emph{multi-hop} stratum), 30 comparison questions
(\emph{multi-passage}), 15 bridge or comparison questions with short contexts
(4--19 sentences against a median of 40; the \emph{short-context} stratum,
which is not a verified single-hop set), and 8 synthetic questions about
fictional entities with no support in the corpus (\emph{unanswerable}).
Gold answers are the HotpotQA strings (mean 2.5 words) without aliases.
The corpus pools the distractor-setting paragraphs of the 145 HotpotQA
questions and splits them into 5{,}675 sentence-level passages
(\texttt{title::s$i$}), so each question competes with passages from the
other 144 questions as well as its own distractors.
Retrieval is BM25 ($k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$) over title and sentence text with
\texttt{top\_k}\,$=$\,5 per call.
The gold evidence of a question is the set of all sentences of its supporting
paragraphs.

\paragraph{Model and budgets.}
All calls use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} at temperature 0 with at most 512 output
tokens.
The main runs (2026-09-15) requested the API alias and did not record the
returned model identifier, so their exact snapshot cannot be recovered; the
control runs (2026-09-30) and all judge calls recorded
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18}.
The \texttt{unconstrained} budget is 8 steps, 12 model calls, 5 retrieval
calls, 20 documents, and 8{,}000 tokens per question.
Each mode was run once per architecture, and no call failed.
The multi-agent critic's verdict never matched the controller's early-stop
pattern, so every question received three retrieval rounds and five model
calls: the pipeline behaved as a fixed three-round schedule.
Because RAG reads five passages in both modes, the equal-retrieval mode
changes the effective budget only for the single-agent loop and is treated as
a replication of the unconstrained run.

\paragraph{Passage-budget control.}
Because three deduplicated rounds of five passages give the multi-agent
pipeline far more text than RAG, RAG was re-run with \texttt{top\_k}\,$=$\,15
(the pipeline's maximum) and \texttt{top\_k}\,$=$\,13 (close to its mean)
on the same 153 questions, with the same corpus, retriever, prompt, model, and
decoding settings; only \texttt{top\_k} differs.
RAG consults neither retrieval-call nor document caps, so its two modes are
procedurally identical (the two top-5 runs retrieve identical passages for
all 153 questions), and one run per \texttt{top\_k} serves both modes.
The top-5 results are the original runs.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Answers are normalized in the HotpotQA style.
\emph{Lenient EM} (\lem{}) is 1 if the normalized prediction equals the gold
answer or either contains the other; \emph{contains-gold} (\cg{}) requires
the prediction to contain the gold (reverse containment only for strings of
at most five tokens).
Because \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} answers in full sentences (30--35 words against
2.5-word gold answers), strict EM is 0.000 for every system and token F1 is
dominated by length; \lem{} and \cg{} (which differ on one question in the
main runs) are therefore the primary string metrics, and \emph{soft F1}
(token F1 raised to 0.5 when \cg{}$\,=1$) is secondary.
String metrics are 0 on the unanswerable questions, so the maximum \lem{} is
$145/153 = 0.948$.
\emph{Evidence recall} is the fraction of gold-evidence sentences retrieved at
any point, and \emph{all support} is the fraction of answerable questions
with at least one retrieved sentence from every supporting paragraph; both are
recomputed from the traces (the harness field \texttt{recall@k} agrees for
RAG at every \texttt{top\_k} and for the multi-agent pipeline, but reports a
spurious 1.0 instead of 0.383 for the single-agent runner, whose retrieved
IDs it does not find).
Cost comprises model and retrieval calls, unique passages, total tokens,
wall-clock latency, and an estimated USD cost at list prices (USD 0.15 input
and 0.60 output per million tokens) with each question's tokens split equally
between input and output; this is a rough estimate, not billing data.

\paragraph{LLM judge and audit.}
Because containment can credit an answer that mentions the gold without
committing to it and misses paraphrases, every saved answer is also graded by
an LLM judge (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, temperature 0, JSON output,
prompt version \texttt{judge-v2}); no system is re-run.
The judge sees only the question, the gold answer, and the answer, never the
architecture, and requests are issued in a seeded shuffled order.
For answerable questions an answer is correct only if it commits to the gold
entity or value (aliases, formatting differences, and paraphrases accepted)
and incorrect if it hedges, names another entity, mentions the gold only in
passing, or abstains.
Unanswerable questions use a separate rubric under which an answer is correct
if and only if it abstains.
An earlier prompt combining both rubrics credited abstentions on answerable
questions and was replaced before the audit.
All 1{,}224 judgments returned a valid verdict (918 for the main runs, using
313{,}858 prompt and 17{,}181 completion tokens, about USD 0.06; 306 for the
control runs, about USD 0.02).
To check the judge, I audited 60 judgments drawn with a fixed seed from
answerable questions in the unconstrained run, spread evenly over the
architectures: 30 on which the judge and \lem{} disagree and 30 on which they
agree.
An AI assistant produced initial correctness labels, which I then reviewed
item by item against the gold answers and model outputs.
The judge agreed with the audit on 54 of 60 items (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.78$)
and \lem{} on 36 of 60 ($\kappa = 0.23$); reweighting the strata to their
frequency in the run gives an estimated judge--audit agreement of 96.8\% on
answerable questions.
In four of the six disagreements the judge accepted an answer that does not
match the gold, twice by overruling an apparently wrong gold label with outside
knowledge and twice by accepting a loosely related answer to a gold string
that is a sentence fragment; in the other two it rejected ``over 300{,}000''
for the gold ``300{,}000''.
The control verdicts were not formally audited; a spot check is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:control}.

\paragraph{Statistics and reproduction.}
Confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap 95\% intervals
\citep{efron1993bootstrap} with 10{,}000 resamples over questions (seed 42);
differences between systems use a paired bootstrap over the same questions,
with discordant counts (questions correct for only one of the two systems).
The four runs are defined by the \path{live_hotpot150_*.yaml} files in
\path{configs/experiments/}; the slice, corpus, and build metadata (seed and
SHA-256 of the source file) are in \path{fixtures/}.
\begin{verbatim}
pip install -e ".[dev,openai]"
python scripts/run_experiment.py --config <config.yaml>
python scripts/judge_answers.py [--runs <run dirs>]  # cached verdicts
python analysis/compute_tables.py --ars-root <path-to-ARS>
\end{verbatim}
The last script recomputes every number, table, and figure from the saved
results, traces, cached verdicts, and audit file without API calls.

\subsection{Passage-budget control}
\label{sec:control}

The multi-agent pipeline read 13.1 unique passages per question in both modes
(unconstrained: median 14, range 10--15, with 15 passages on 47 of 153
questions; equal-retrieval: mean 13.1, median 13), against 5.0 for RAG.
Table~\ref{tab:control} gives the control.
Raising RAG's \texttt{top\_k} from 5 to 15 increases judge accuracy from
0.601 to 0.752 ($+0.150$, CI $+0.092$ to $+0.216$; 25 vs.\ 2 discordant) and
\lem{} from 0.510 to 0.641 ($+0.131$, $+0.078$ to $+0.190$; 20 vs.\ 0).
The remaining multi-agent advantage is $+0.013$ under the judge ($-0.033$ to
$+0.059$; 8 vs.\ 6) and $-0.020$ under \lem{} ($-0.059$ to $+0.020$; 3 vs.\
6); soft F1 gives $-0.006$ ($-0.025$ to $+0.013$).
Against the equal-retrieval multi-agent run the judge difference is the same
($+0.013$, $-0.033$ to $+0.059$).
Top-13 RAG gives the same judge accuracy (0.752; multi-agent minus RAG
$+0.013$, $-0.026$ to $+0.052$, 6 vs.\ 4).
Top-15 RAG recovers a share of 0.92 of the multi-agent judge gain (paired
bootstrap 95\% CI 0.67--1.26); for scale, the two identical top-5 RAG runs
differ on 2 questions (1 each way).

By stratum, top-15 RAG gains most on multi-hop questions (0.510 to 0.700,
19 vs.\ 0) and slightly exceeds the multi-agent pipeline there (0.680).
The only stratum with a residual multi-agent advantage is multi-passage
(0.933 vs.\ 0.833; $+0.100$, $-0.033$ to $+0.233$; 4 vs.\ 1), where $n{=}30$
leaves the interval wide.
Top-15 RAG reaches evidence recall 0.618 and all-support coverage 0.786,
close to the multi-agent pipeline (0.641 and 0.766; Table~\ref{tab:diag}),
and abstains on 5 of 145 answerable questions (top-5 RAG: 24).

The longer context (775 instead of 296 prompt tokens on average) did not
measurably hurt RAG.
Only 2 questions correct at top-5 were judged incorrect at top-15 (none under
\lem{}): one answer claims two singers released equally many albums, and in
the other the same substantive answer (``Terry Kath was born first'') to a question with
an apparently wrong gold label was accepted for top-5 RAG and the multi-agent
pipeline but rejected for top-15, an inconsistency of the judge.
Among answerable questions with every supporting paragraph retrieved, judge

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The multi-agent gain is an evidence-budget effect.}
With RAG at five passages, the multi-agent pipeline looked clearly better on
multi-hop and multi-passage questions, and it retrieved far more of the gold
evidence.
The control isolates that difference: a comparable passage budget in a single
retrieval call recovers about nine-tenths of the judge gain and matches the
pipeline on multi-hop questions at 30\% of the tokens and about one-fortieth
of the latency.
Planning, role-separated retrieval, and critique therefore add little here
beyond the passages they bring in; a residual advantage on comparison
questions (4 vs.\ 1 discordant) is possible but not established.
The setup favours a large \texttt{top\_k}: the pooled corpus consists of each
question's own paragraphs plus distractors, so BM25 on the original question
already ranks much of the evidence within the top 15, and sentence-level
passages keep the context short.
Despite known long-context degradation \citep{liu2024lost}, tripling the
context did not reduce accuracy on questions whose evidence was already
retrieved.
With full-Wikipedia retrieval, paragraph-length passages, or evidence that
the original question does not surface, query decomposition may matter more;
such claims need passage-budget-matched baselines.

\paragraph{Why the single-agent loop does not help.}
The loop rarely iterates, so its evidence coverage stays close to RAG's, its
extra calls go mostly to control decisions, and it abstains more often.
This concerns this controller prompt and model, not iterative retrieval in
general \citep{trivedi2023ircot,jiang2023flare}.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
The gap between \lem{} ($\approx$0.5--0.65) and token F1 ($\approx$0.1)
reflects answer verbosity: the model usually embeds a correct short answer in
a sentence.
Containment over-credits hedged answers and misses paraphrases; the judge
corrects most errors of both kinds in the audit and changes no conclusion.
Constrained short-answer output would make strict EM and token F1
informative.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All results use one model (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, temperature 0), and rankings
may differ for others.
The slice is a stratified 153-question sample plus 8 synthetic unanswerable
questions; the short-context stratum is not verified single-hop, and the
numbers are not comparable to HotpotQA leaderboard results.
With 30, 15, and 8 questions in the smaller strata, per-stratum conclusions
beyond multi-hop are tentative.
Retrieval runs over a pooled sentence-level corpus rather than full
Wikipedia, and only BM25 is evaluated (RQ4 is open).
The string metrics are lenient, and the judge is the same model as the
systems under test, may share their biases, sometimes overrules apparently
wrong gold labels with outside knowledge (and does so inconsistently, as in
the control), and was audited on 60 items whose labels were drafted by an AI
assistant and reviewed by a single author; the control verdicts were only
spot-checked.
The equal-retrieval mode does not equalize passages read, the multi-agent
critic never stopped early (so planning and critique cannot be ablated
separately from the three-round schedule), the equal-token mode was not run,
and the control varies RAG's \texttt{top\_k} but not the multi-agent
pipeline's.
Each configuration was run once, and the control ran two weeks after the main
runs, whose model snapshot is unknown; identical retrieval, the small
run-to-run difference, and the shared judge snapshot limit how much this can
matter.
USD figures are estimates, and citation precision and recall are not reported
because the harness did not parse the model's citation format.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

ARS compares conventional RAG, bounded iterative retrieval, and multi-agent
retrieval under a shared corpus, budget controller, trace format, and metric
suite.
On a 153-question HotpotQA-derived slice with \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and BM25,
a three-round multi-agent pipeline outperformed five-passage RAG, but RAG
given 15 passages in one call matched it at about a third of the tokens and a
small fraction of the latency, and the single-agent loop added cost without a
quality gain.
Future work includes passage-budget matching as a default baseline and an
equal-token run; a critic whose output the controller parses, so that early
stopping and critique can be ablated; constrained short-answer output and
citation parsing; additional models, the full HotpotQA development set, and
2WikiMultiHopQA; and dense and hybrid retrieval for RQ4.
