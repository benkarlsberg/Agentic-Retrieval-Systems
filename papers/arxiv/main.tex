\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems increasingly add iterative and
multi-agent control loops, but comparisons across these architectures are
often confounded by differences in corpora, budgets, logging, and metrics.
We present \textbf{Agentic Retrieval Systems (ARS)}, an open-source harness
that runs conventional retrieve-once RAG, a budgeted single-agent iterative
retriever, and a role-separated multi-agent pipeline against a shared corpus,
model interface, metric suite, and budget controller, and records structured
per-question traces.
We evaluate the three architectures with \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} on a
153-question slice derived from the HotpotQA distractor development set
(100 bridge, 30 comparison, 15 short-context, and 8 synthetic unanswerable
questions) using BM25 over a pooled corpus of 5{,}675 sentence-level passages,
under an unconstrained and an equal-retrieval budget mode.
In the unconstrained run, the multi-agent pipeline reaches a lenient exact
match of 0.621 (95\% bootstrap CI 0.542--0.693), compared with 0.510 for RAG
and 0.497 for the single-agent loop; the paired multi-agent minus RAG
difference is $+0.111$ (95\% CI $+0.059$ to $+0.163$).
The gain is concentrated on bridge (multi-hop) and comparison
(multi-passage) questions and comes at 8.2$\times$ the tokens of RAG
(2{,}744 vs.\ 336 per question) and five model calls instead of one.
In the same run, the single-agent loop uses 2.7$\times$ the tokens of RAG
with no measurable quality gain.
The equal-retrieval run reproduces these results.
The study is limited to one model, a small derived slice, and a local BM25
corpus; it is not a HotpotQA leaderboard evaluation.
Code, configurations, and analysis scripts are available at
\url{https://github.com/benkarlsberg/Agentic-Retrieval-Systems}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open-domain question answering and related knowledge-intensive tasks
increasingly rely on retrieval-augmented generation
\citep{lewis2020rag,karpukhin2020dpr,gao2023ragsurvey}.
Beyond the classical retrieve-once pattern, recent systems interleave
retrieval with reasoning \citep{trivedi2023ircot,jiang2023flare,yao2023react},
reflect on evidence quality \citep{asai2023selfrag}, and route queries by
complexity \citep{jeong2024adaptiverag}.
In parallel, multi-agent designs split planning, retrieval, critique, and
synthesis across specialized roles.
These designs are motivated by multi-hop and multi-passage questions, where a
single retrieval round may miss part of the required evidence, but they add
model calls, retrieval calls, tokens, and latency.

Comparing such architectures fairly is difficult.
Published systems rarely share corpora, budget caps, logging schemas, and
metric definitions, so an observed quality gap may reflect implementation
choices rather than architecture.
A useful comparison therefore needs a harness that holds these factors fixed
and reports process cost alongside answer quality.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
The framework is organized around four questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Under matched retrieval or token budgets, does bounded
    iterative retrieval improve answer quality over retrieve-once RAG on
    multi-hop / multi-passage items?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does role-separated multi-agent retrieval improve
    evidence coverage and answer quality enough to justify its coordination
    overhead?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How do architectures differ on unanswerable questions
    (abstention vs.\ false answers)?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] How sensitive are architecture rankings to sparse
    (BM25), dense, and hybrid retrieval under a fixed corpus?
\end{enumerate}
This paper reports evidence on RQ1 and RQ2, preliminary evidence on RQ3
(8 unanswerable items), and no evidence on RQ4, since all live runs use BM25.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A reproducible harness that runs conventional RAG, a bounded
    single-agent loop, and a multi-agent pipeline with shared retrieval,
    budgets, metrics, and JSONL tracing, including budget-matched comparison
    modes and logged termination reasons.
  \item A live comparison of the three architectures with
    \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} on a 153-question HotpotQA-derived slice under two
    budget modes, reporting answer quality with bootstrap confidence
    intervals, paired differences, per-category results, and per-question
    cost (tokens, model and retrieval calls, latency, estimated USD).
  \item A quality--cost analysis showing that, in this setting, the
    multi-agent pipeline improves lenient exact match by about 11 points over
    RAG, mainly on bridge and comparison questions, at roughly eight times the
    token cost, while the single-agent loop gives no measurable gain over RAG
    at 2.6--2.7$\times$ its token cost.
  \item An open-source release of the code, run configurations, and the
    analysis script that regenerates every table and figure in this paper
    from the run artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Retrieve-and-read and dense retrieval.}
Classical open-domain QA pipelines retrieve passages and then read them for
answers \citep{chen2017drqa}.
Dense Passage Retrieval \citep{karpukhin2020dpr} and RAG
\citep{lewis2020rag} popularized neural retrieval combined with generation.
Sparse baselines such as BM25 remain strong and widely used
\citep{robertson2009bm25}.
Hybrid fusion (e.g., reciprocal rank fusion) often combines sparse and dense
signals \citep{cormack2009rrf}.

\paragraph{Iterative and adaptive retrieval.}
IRCoT interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning for multi-step
questions \citep{trivedi2023ircot}.
FLARE triggers retrieval when generation confidence is low
\citep{jiang2023flare}.
Self-RAG trains models to decide when to retrieve and how to critique
evidence \citep{asai2023selfrag}.
Adaptive-RAG routes queries by estimated complexity
\citep{jeong2024adaptiverag}.
ReAct and related tool-using agents couple reasoning with actions such as
search \citep{yao2023react,schick2023toolformer,press2023selfask}.
ARS does not propose a new retrieval policy; it provides a controlled
setting in which such policies can be compared under shared budgets.

\paragraph{Multi-hop benchmarks.}
HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, Natural Questions
\citep{kwiatkowski2019nq}, and 2WikiMultiHopQA \citep{ho2020twowiki}
are standard evaluation resources.
Our live study uses a small stratified slice of the HotpotQA distractor
development set with a local retrieval corpus (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
Its numbers are not comparable to the official HotpotQA leaderboard, which
uses the full development and test sets and a different answer-scoring
protocol.

\paragraph{Evaluation methodology.}
Surveys of RAG emphasize the need for systematic metrics spanning answer
quality, retrieval, faithfulness, and cost \citep{gao2023ragsurvey}.
ARS provides typed schemas, per-question traces, and bootstrap confidence
intervals \citep{efron1993bootstrap} for these measurements.

\section{Framework Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Architectures}
\label{sec:arch}

ARS implements three runners that share the same corpus, retriever, model
client, budget controller, and metric suite.

\paragraph{A --- Conventional RAG.}
One retrieval call with the original question returns \texttt{top\_k}
passages; one generation call produces the answer or an abstention.

\paragraph{B --- Bounded single-agent.}
At each step a controller call chooses to \emph{retrieve},
\emph{reformulate} the query, \emph{stop} (and synthesize), or
\emph{abstain}.
Retrieved passages are deduplicated against earlier steps.
Hard caps on steps, model calls, retrieval calls, documents, and tokens
bound the loop, and the stop reason is logged.

\paragraph{C --- Multi-agent.}
A planner call decomposes the question; two retriever roles (``fact'' and
``evidence'') alternate over up to three retrieval rounds, each excluding
passages already retrieved; a critic call after each round judges whether the
evidence is sufficient; and a synthesizer call produces the final answer or
an abstention.
Coordination messages are recorded in the trace.

\subsection{Shared retrieval stack}
Retrievers include Okapi BM25 (with a pure-Python fallback), dense retrieval
over hashing-based fixture embeddings (not a trained encoder), hybrid RRF
fusion, and an optional lexical reranker.
The live study in this paper uses BM25 only.

\subsection{Budgets and comparison modes}
Budgets limit steps, model calls, documents, tokens, retrieval calls, and
\texttt{top\_k}.
Comparison modes rewrite the per-architecture budget:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \texttt{unconstrained}: the configured budget, still hard-capped;
  \item \texttt{equal\_retrieval}: at most 3 retrieval calls and 15
    documents for every architecture, with RAG restricted to one retrieval
    call and one model call;
  \item \texttt{equal\_token}: a shared token cap (not run in this study).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tracing}
Every question emits JSONL events covering issued queries, retrieved passage
IDs and scores, routing actions, token accounting, latency, estimated cost,
and termination reason.
An HTML viewer supports qualitative inspection of individual traces.

\subsection{Model interface}
A provider-neutral client supports a deterministic heuristic model
(\texttt{DeterministicModel}) for offline tests, and OpenAI-compatible live
backends configured through environment variables.
Token counts in live runs are taken from the API usage fields.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset slice}
\label{sec:data}
We derive a 153-question evaluation slice from the HotpotQA distractor
development set (\texttt{hotpot\_dev\_distractor\_v1.json}, 7{,}405
questions, all labelled \emph{hard}) with seeded sampling (seed 42).
Table~\ref{tab:slice} lists the strata.
Multi-hop items are HotpotQA \emph{bridge} questions and multi-passage items
are \emph{comparison} questions.
HotpotQA does not annotate hop count; the 15 items in the single-hop stratum
are bridge (9) and comparison (6) questions with short contexts
(4--19 sentences, against a median of 40 in the development set; 9 of them
have fewer than the usual ten paragraphs), so this stratum is better read as
``short-context'' than as guaranteed single-hop.
The 8 unanswerable questions are synthetic questions about fictional
entities (e.g., ``What is the capital of the Republic of Middle
Earthia?'') with no support in the corpus.
Gold answers are the HotpotQA answer strings (mean length 2.5 words); no
answer aliases are used.

\subsection{Corpus and retrieval}
The retrieval corpus is the union of the distractor-setting context
paragraphs of the 145 HotpotQA questions (the two supporting paragraphs plus
distractors; ten paragraphs for 135 questions and two to five for the other
ten), split into sentences.
Each sentence is one passage with ID \texttt{title::s$i$}, giving 5{,}675
passages.
Retrieval is Okapi BM25 ($k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$) over title and sentence
text, with \texttt{top\_k}\,$=$\,5 passages per call.
Because distractor paragraphs from all questions are pooled, each question
competes against passages from the other 144 questions as well as its own
distractors.
For retrieval diagnostics, the gold evidence of a question is the set of all
sentences of its supporting paragraphs.

\subsection{Model and budgets}
All model calls use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} through the OpenAI API with
temperature 0 and at most 512 output tokens per call.
In the \texttt{unconstrained} mode the budget is 8 steps, 12 model calls,
5 retrieval calls, 20 documents, and 8{,}000 tokens per question.
In the \texttt{equal\_retrieval} mode the single-agent and multi-agent
runners are limited to 3 retrieval calls and 15 documents, and RAG to one
retrieval and one model call.
Each mode was run once over all 153 questions for each architecture
(459 questions per mode); no call failed.

Two properties of the runs affect how the modes should be read.
First, the multi-agent critic's verdict never matched the controller's
early-stop pattern, so every question received exactly three retrieval
rounds followed by synthesis (5 model calls and 3 retrieval calls for all
153 questions in both modes); the multi-agent condition therefore behaved as
a fixed three-round pipeline.
Second, RAG uses a single top-5 retrieval in both modes.
The equal-retrieval mode thus caps retrieval calls but does not equalize the
number of passages each architecture actually reads, and it changes the
effective budget only for the single-agent loop.
We therefore treat the equal-retrieval run primarily as a replication of the
unconstrained run.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Answers are normalized in the HotpotQA style (lower-casing and removing
punctuation, articles, and extra whitespace).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Lenient EM (\lem{})}: 1 if the normalized prediction equals
    the normalized gold answer or either string contains the other
    (the harness field \texttt{em}).
  \item \textbf{Contains-gold (\cg{})}: 1 if the normalized prediction
    contains the normalized gold answer (reverse containment is accepted only
    for short strings of at most five tokens).
  \item \textbf{Strict EM}: normalized string equality (harness field
    \texttt{soft\_em}).
  \item \textbf{Token F1}: SQuAD-style token-overlap F1 against the gold
    answer.
  \item \textbf{Soft F1}: token F1, raised to 0.5 when \cg{}$\,=1$.
  \item \textbf{Abstention}: whether the system abstains on unanswerable
    questions and answers the answerable ones.
  \item \textbf{Evidence recall}: the fraction of gold-evidence sentences
    retrieved at any point for the question, and the fraction of questions
    for which at least one sentence of every supporting paragraph was
    retrieved.
    We recompute both from the traces for all
    architectures.\footnote{The harness's own \texttt{recall@k} field matches
    our recomputation for RAG and multi-agent, but for the single-agent
    runner it falls back to the gold IDs because that runner logs retrieved
    IDs in a different trace event, and it reports a spurious 1.0.}
  \item \textbf{Cost}: model calls, retrieval calls, unique passages read,
    total tokens (prompt plus completion), wall-clock latency, and an
    estimated USD cost.
\end{itemize}
Answer metrics are 0 on the 8 unanswerable questions, and all means are over
the full 153 questions, so the maximum attainable \lem{} is
$145/153 = 0.948$.
Because \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} answers in full sentences (30--34 words on
average for non-abstained answers, against 2.5-word gold answers), strict EM
is 0.000 for every architecture in both runs and token F1 is heavily
penalized by answer length.
We therefore use \lem{} and \cg{} as the primary quality metrics, report
soft F1 as a secondary graded measure, and report token F1 only for
completeness.
\lem{} and \cg{} coincide on every question except one (single-agent,
equal-retrieval).

The estimated cost uses list prices of USD 0.15 (input) and USD 0.60 (output)
per million tokens, and the harness splits each question's total tokens
equally between input and output; it is a rough estimate, not billing data.
Latency is end-to-end wall-clock time including API round trips.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
We report percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals
\citep{efron1993bootstrap} with 10{,}000 resamples over questions.
Architecture differences are estimated with a paired bootstrap over the
same questions; we also report discordant counts, i.e., the number of
questions answered correctly by one architecture but not the other.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Overall quality}

Table~\ref{tab:quality} reports answer quality for both modes.
In the unconstrained run, the multi-agent pipeline reaches \lem{} 0.621,
against 0.510 for RAG and 0.497 for the single-agent loop.
The paired multi-agent minus RAG difference is $+0.111$
(95\% CI $+0.059$ to $+0.163$); the multi-agent pipeline answers 18
questions correctly that RAG misses, and RAG answers 1 question correctly that
the multi-agent pipeline misses.
The single-agent minus RAG difference is $-0.013$ ($-0.065$ to $+0.039$;
7 vs.\ 9 discordant questions), i.e., no detectable difference.
Soft F1 gives the same ordering (multi-agent minus RAG $+0.054$, CI
$+0.029$ to $+0.080$; single-agent minus RAG $-0.009$, CI $-0.034$ to
$+0.016$).

The equal-retrieval run gives nearly identical results: multi-agent minus RAG
is $+0.118$ ($+0.072$ to $+0.170$; 18 vs.\ 0 discordant) and single-agent
minus RAG is $+0.013$ ($-0.039$ to $+0.065$; 10 vs.\ 8).
Per-question \lem{} agrees between the two runs on 99.3\% (RAG), 96.7\%
(single-agent), and 97.4\% (multi-agent) of questions.
Since the RAG pipeline and its retrieval are identical in both modes, the
disagreements for RAG reflect residual non-determinism of the API at
temperature 0.

\subsection{Cost}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports process cost.
RAG uses one model call and 336 tokens per question.
The single-agent loop uses 2.99 model calls and 892 tokens
(2.7$\times$ RAG), and the multi-agent pipeline uses 5 model calls and
2{,}744 tokens (8.2$\times$ RAG), and it reads 13.1 unique passages per
question against 5.0 for RAG.
Estimated cost for the 153 questions is USD 0.019 (RAG), 0.051
(single-agent), and 0.157 (multi-agent).
Within the unconstrained run, median latency is 0.77\,s for RAG, 19.8\,s for
the single-agent loop, and 41.7\,s for the multi-agent pipeline.
Latency is less stable than the other cost measures: RAG's mean latency was
0.81\,s in the unconstrained run and 8.57\,s in the equal-retrieval run with
an identical pipeline, so latencies should be compared only within a run.

\subsection{Evidence coverage and abstention}

Table~\ref{tab:diag} reports retrieval and abstention diagnostics for the
unconstrained run.
The multi-agent pipeline retrieves 64.1\% of gold-evidence sentences and
covers every supporting paragraph for 76.6\% of answerable questions,
against 36.6\% and 48.3\% for RAG.
The single-agent loop retrieves little more than RAG (38.3\% and 50.3\%);
it issued a single retrieval call on 101 of 153 questions and none on 24.
On 16 answerable questions the single-agent controller abstained without
retrieving anything.
It abstains on 31 of 145 answerable questions, RAG on 24, and the
multi-agent pipeline on 8.
On the unanswerable items, RAG and the single-agent loop abstain on all 8
and the multi-agent pipeline on 6.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the multi-agent pipeline helps.}
The multi-agent gain is clearest on the bridge and comparison strata, where
answering requires combining evidence from two paragraphs and where the
strata are large enough for the paired intervals to exclude zero.
It coincides with higher evidence coverage (Table~\ref{tab:diag}): with
three deduplicated retrieval rounds the pipeline reads about 13 sentences
instead of 5, and far more often retrieves material from both supporting
paragraphs.
It also abstains less often on answerable questions (8 vs.\ 24 for RAG).
These observations are consistent with the view that the gain comes mainly
from reading more, and more diverse, evidence rather than from role
separation itself.
Because the critic never stopped early in these runs, the study cannot
separate the contribution of planning and critique from that of a fixed
three-round retrieval schedule; an ablation that gives RAG a comparable
passage budget (e.g., \texttt{top\_k}\,$=$\,15) would be needed.
The pipeline's lower abstention rate has a cost on unanswerable questions,
where it answered 2 of 8 instead of abstaining.

\paragraph{Why the single-agent loop does not help.}
In this configuration the single-agent loop rarely iterates: 101 of 153
questions received exactly one retrieval call and 24 received none.
Its evidence coverage is therefore close to RAG's, and its additional model
calls are spent mostly on control decisions rather than on retrieval.
It also abstains more often on answerable questions than RAG, including 16
questions on which it abstained before retrieving anything.
The loop consequently adds tokens and latency without improving answer
quality.
This finding concerns this controller prompt and this model; it does not
show that iterative retrieval is ineffective in general
\citep{trivedi2023ircot,jiang2023flare}.

\paragraph{Quality--cost trade-off.}
Viewed as a Pareto problem, RAG and the multi-agent pipeline are both
efficient choices here, and which one to use depends on the value of about
11 additional correct answers per 100 questions relative to roughly
8$\times$ the tokens, 5$\times$ the model calls, and tens of seconds of
added latency per question.
At the list prices used here the absolute cost difference is small
(about USD 0.14 per 153 questions), so latency and API rate limits may matter
more than token cost in practice.
The single-agent loop, as configured, is not on the unconstrained frontier.

\paragraph{Answer format and metrics.}
The gap between \lem{} ($\approx$0.5--0.6) and token F1 ($\approx$0.1) is
caused by answer verbosity, not by retrieval: the model typically embeds the
correct short answer in a full sentence.
Containment-based metrics credit these answers, but they can also credit a
long answer that mentions the gold string without committing to it.
Constraining the output format or extracting a short answer span would allow
standard strict EM and token F1 to be used.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{One model.} All live results use \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} at
    temperature 0; rankings may differ for other models.
  \item \textbf{Derived slice, not the full benchmark.} The 153 questions
    are a stratified sample of the HotpotQA distractor development set plus
    8 synthetic unanswerable questions, and the short-context stratum is not
    a verified single-hop set. The numbers are not comparable to HotpotQA
    leaderboard results.
  \item \textbf{Primary metrics are lenient.} Raw token F1 is depressed by
    verbose answers, and strict EM is 0 for every architecture because
    normalized string equality almost never holds for sentence-length
    answers. We therefore rely on \lem{} and \cg{}, which are containment
    based and can over-credit long answers.
  \item \textbf{Small strata.} With 30, 15, and 8 questions in the smaller
    strata, confidence intervals are wide, and per-category conclusions
    beyond the multi-hop stratum are tentative.
  \item \textbf{Local BM25 corpus.} Retrieval runs over a pooled corpus of
    sentence-level passages built from the distractor paragraphs, not over
    full Wikipedia, and only BM25 is evaluated (RQ4 is not addressed).
  \item \textbf{Budget matching.} The equal-retrieval mode caps retrieval
    calls but does not equalize passages read, and the multi-agent critic
    never triggered an early stop; the equal-token mode was not run.
  \item \textbf{Single runs.} Each configuration was run once; the
    agreement between the two modes suggests low but non-zero run-to-run
    variation.
  \item \textbf{Cost and citations.} USD figures are estimates from list
    prices and an assumed equal input/output token split. The harness did
    not parse the citation format produced by the model, so citation
    precision and recall are not reported.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented ARS, a harness for comparing conventional RAG, bounded
iterative retrieval, and multi-agent retrieval under a shared corpus,
budget controller, trace format, and metric suite.
On a 153-question HotpotQA-derived slice with \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and BM25,
a three-round multi-agent pipeline improved lenient exact match over RAG by
about 11 points, mainly on bridge and comparison questions, at about eight
times the token cost and a large latency increase, while the single-agent
loop added cost without a quality gain.
Both findings replicated under an equal-retrieval budget.

\paragraph{Future work.}
(1)~Passage-budget-matched baselines (RAG with larger \texttt{top\_k}) and
the equal-token mode;
(2)~a critic output format that the controller parses, so that early
stopping and critique can be ablated;
(3)~constrained short-answer output so that strict EM and token F1 are
informative, and citation parsing;
(4)~additional models, the full HotpotQA development set, and
2WikiMultiHopQA;
(5)~dense and hybrid retrieval for RQ4.

\section{Pipeline Validation}
\label{app:validation}

Before the live study, the harness was checked in two small runs on the
checked-in mini fixtures (24 passages, $n{=}13$ questions covering
single-hop, multi-hop, multi-passage, ambiguous, and unanswerable items;
BM25, top-5, unconstrained).
With the heuristic \texttt{DeterministicModel}, all three architectures
obtained the same \lem{} (0.846) and abstention correctness (1.000), as
expected for a shared extractive heuristic, while cost increased with
architectural complexity (1.00, 3.00, and 4.31 model calls; 294, 654, and
1{,}625 mean tokens for RAG, single-agent, and multi-agent).
A live \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} smoke run on the same 13 questions gave \lem{}
0.692 for all three architectures, with 1.00, 4.23, and 5.00 model calls and
300, 1{,}460, and 2{,}331 mean tokens.
An offline equal-retrieval run also completed.
These runs verified that the runners, budgets, traces, and metrics operate
end to end; with $n{=}13$ they carry no information about relative answer
quality.
